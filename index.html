Advancing Medical Representation Learning Through High‑Quality Data

Introducing Open‑PMC-2M: a curated medical image–text dataset of 2.2M pairs with subfigures, enriched in‑text references, and modality labels.

arXiv: 2503.14377 • 18 Mar 2025 Multimodal Learning Representation Learning

Negin Baghbanzadeh*, Adibvafa Fallahpour*, Yasaman Parhizkar*, Franklin Ogidi, Shuvendu Roy, Sajad Ashkezari, Vahid Reza Khazaie, Michael Colacci, Ali Etemad, Arash Afkanpour†, and Elham Dolatabadi†

York University • Vector Institute • University of Toronto • Queen’s University • University Health Network

Key Takeaways

  • Open‑PMC-2M dataset: 2.2 M image–text pairs extracted from PubMed Central, where each image is a subfigure and each text combines captions, subcaptions and summarized in‑text references.
  • Broad modality coverage: every pair is labeled as Radiology, Microscopy or Visible‑Light Photography (VLP) and non‑medical content is filtered out.
  • Quality over quantity: despite being 3–7× smaller than PMC‑15M and Biomedica, models trained on Open‑PMC achieve the highest Average Recall and Mean Reciprocal Rank across retrieval benchmarks and the best mean F1 in radiology zero‑shot classification.
  • Ablation insights: replacing compound figures with subfigures and adding summarized in‑text references are key drivers of the performance gains seen with Open‑PMC-2M.

Abstract

Despite the growing scale of medical Vision‑Language datasets, the impact of dataset quality on model performance remains under‑explored. We introduce Open‑PMC-2M, a high‑quality medical dataset from PubMed Central, containing 2.2 million image‑text pairs, enriched with image modality annotations, subfigures, and summarized in‑text references. The in‑text references provide richer medical context than captions alone. We benchmark Open‑PMC-2M against larger datasets across retrieval and zero‑shot classification tasks, showing that data quality, not just size, drives strong gains. We include feature‑space analysis and release data, trained models, and code.

Overview

We study how curation quality versus quantity affects learned representations in medical vision‑language models. To explore whether targeted curation improves representation learning, we decompose compound figures into subfigures, break captions into segments, and add summarized in‑text references; our experiments show these steps lead to better learned representations. We evaluate encoders across radiology, microscopy, and visible‑light tasks.

Contributions

  • Open‑PMC-2M dataset: 2.2 M image–text pairs from PubMed Central, each consisting of a subfigure and a text that combines captions, subcaptions, and summarized in‑text references; every pair is labeled as radiology, microscopy or visible‑light photography.
  • Curation pipeline: We split compound figures into individual subfigures, align them with their subcaptions, add summaries of in‑text references, filter out non‑medical images, and assign modalities, with human checks to ensure quality.
  • Quality vs. quantity: On retrieval and zero‑shot classification benchmarks across radiology, microscopy and VLP, the Open‑PMC encoder outperforms models trained on much larger datasets like BIIOMEDICA and PMC‑15M.
  • Ablation findings: Experiments show that replacing compound figures with subfigures and including in‑text summaries each contribute to the observed performance gains.
  • Representation analysis: MMD tests and t‑SNE visualisations reveal that Open‑PMC-2M encoders learn a different latent space compared with models trained on other datasets.
  • Public release: The dataset, trained encoders and codebase are publicly available for the research community.

Dataset

Composition

  • 2.2M image–text pairs after rigorous filtering.
  • Images are subfigures from compound figures.
  • Texts include captions, subcaptions, and summarized in‑text references.
  • Modalities: Radiology, Microscopy, Visible‑Light Photography (VLP).

Curation Steps (brief)

  1. Data extraction & linking — Processed over four million open‑access articles with the PMC‑OA pipeline, parsing XML to extract figures, captions and paragraphs that reference them, then linking each figure–caption pair with article metadata such as PMID and PMC‑ID.
  2. Quality control — Removed articles with malformed XML or missing captions and filtered out figure–caption pairs lacking medical keywords to build an intermediate Open‑PMC‑17M dataset:contentReference[oaicite:1]{index=1}.
  3. Compound figure decomposition — Used a DETR‑based detector trained on MedICaT to split compound figures into individual subfigures, then a ResNet‑101 model trained on DocFigure to discard non‑medical images, yielding 2.2 M high‑quality medical subfigures.
  4. Caption segmentation & alignment — Employed GPT‑4o to break each full caption into subcaptions. Object‑localization and text‑recognition models (YOLOv3 and ResNet‑152) from Exsclaim matched panel labels to the correct subcaption; if labels were missing, the full caption was assigned.
  5. In‑text reference summarization — Extracted paragraphs mentioning each figure using XML cross‑reference tags and regular expressions, then used GPT‑4o‑mini to produce concise summaries that capture the most relevant clinical context.
  6. Modality assignment & validation — A GPT‑4o‑mini model classified each image as radiology, microscopy, visible‑light photography or non‑diagnostic based on its caption. Three human reviewers checked a random sample of 1 000 images and agreed with the automated labels 87 % of the time.

Results (at a glance)

  • Retrieval (Recall@200): Open‑PMC encoders achieve the best AR and MRR across Quilt, MIMIC‑CXR, and DeepEyeNet.
  • Zero‑shot classification: Highest mean F1 in radiology; competitive across microscopy and VLP.
  • Representation analysis: Distinct latent structure vs. PMC‑15M and Biomedica (MMD test; t‑SNE visuals).

Refer to Table 2 and Figures 2–3 in the paper for full metrics and plots.

Why it matters

In medical AI, scale alone is not enough. Careful curation—subfigure extraction, richer text context, and modality labeling—produces stronger, more reliable representations that transfer better to real tasks.
